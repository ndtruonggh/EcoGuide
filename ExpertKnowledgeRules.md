This file presents the expert knowledge rules used to determine the suitability of environmental factors for the three study species: *Sonneratia caseolaris*, *Rachycentron canadum*, and *Crassostrea gigas*. Each table corresponds to one study species and follows the same structure, consisting of the columns *Environmental Factor*, *Rank*, *Unsuitable*, *Suitable*, and *Highly Suitable*.

The *Environmental Factor* column lists the environmental factors used to assess the suitability of each study species, such as air temperature, rainfall, salinity, pH, nutrients, and heavy metals. The *Rank* column indicates the relative importance of each factor according to expert assessment, where a lower rank indicates greater importance and therefore a higher weight in the score aggregation process.

The *Unsuitable*, *Suitable*, and *Highly Suitable* columns represent the value ranges of each factor corresponding to the three suitability levels: unsuitable, suitable, and highly suitable, respectively. These value ranges are determined based on expert knowledge and assessment thresholds and are used as the basis for constructing the suitability scoring function and classification labels in this study.

Air temperature and seawater temperature influence physiological processes such as photosynthesis, respiration, and metabolic activity, while rainfall contributes to freshwater availability and regulates salinity conditions in coastal environments. Salinity is particularly important for <i>S. caseolaris</i>, as excessive salinity can impose osmotic stress and interfere with water and nutrient uptake.

The physicochemical properties of the aquatic environment, including pH, alkalinity, NH<sub>3</sub>, and H<sub>2</sub>S, also affect environmental suitability. Appropriate pH and alkalinity help maintain a stable chemical environment and influence nutrient availability, whereas elevated concentrations of NH<sub>3</sub> and H<sub>2</sub>S may create toxic conditions and negatively affect plant growth.

In addition, BOD<sub>5</sub>, COD, and TSS are indicators of water quality. High BOD<sub>5</sub> and COD values are generally associated with increased organic pollution and oxygen consumption, while high TSS can increase turbidity and alter light availability and sediment conditions.

Heavy metals, including As, Cd, Pb, Cu, and Zn, are considered potential stressors when their concentrations exceed suitable levels. Although some elements such as Cu and Zn are essential micronutrients at low concentrations, excessive concentrations can cause physiological stress and interfere with metabolic processes. Other contaminants, such as As, Cd, and Pb, can exert toxic effects even at relatively low concentrations.

## Crassostrea gigas

The environmental factors listed in the table above represent the major physicochemical, water-quality, and contamination conditions considered relevant to the growth and development of *Crassostrea gigas*. Dissolved oxygen (DO) is particularly important for oyster respiration and metabolic activity, while seawater temperature affects physiological processes, feeding activity, metabolism, and growth. Salinity influences the osmotic balance of oysters and their ability to maintain normal physiological functions. Air temperature is also considered as a climatic factor that can indirectly affect the thermal conditions of the coastal and aquaculture environment.

The chemical characteristics of the water, including pH and alkalinity, are important for maintaining suitable conditions for oyster growth and shell formation. In particular, pH and alkalinity are closely related to the carbonate chemistry of seawater and therefore affect the availability of carbonate ions required for calcification. Extreme pH conditions can disrupt physiological processes and reduce the ability of oysters to maintain and develop their shells. NH<sub>3</sub> and H<sub>2</sub>S are considered potentially toxic compounds, especially at elevated concentrations, and may adversely affect oyster health and survival.

Transparency and TSS provide information about water clarity and suspended particulate matter. Excessive suspended solids can increase turbidity and interfere with feeding and other biological processes, while transparency provides an indication of the light and particulate conditions of the surrounding water.

BOD<sub>5</sub>, COD, and Coliform are included as indicators of organic pollution and microbiological water quality. High BOD<sub>5</sub> and COD values indicate increased organic loading and oxygen consumption, which may contribute to deteriorated water-quality conditions. Elevated Coliform concentrations indicate greater microbial contamination and may reflect unfavorable sanitary conditions in the culture environment.

Cyanide (CN<sup>-</sup>) is included because of its potential toxicity to aquatic organisms even at relatively low concentrations. Heavy metals, including As, Cd, Pb, Cu, Hg, Zn, and total chromium, are considered potential contaminants that can cause physiological stress and toxic effects when their concentrations exceed suitable levels.

Therefore, the expert-defined thresholds in the table above were used to characterize the environmental conditions into unsuitable, suitable, and highly suitable levels for *C. gigas*.

## Rachycentron canadum

The environmental factors listed in the table above represent the major physicochemical, nutritional, and water-quality conditions considered relevant to the growth and development of *Rachycentron canadum*. Dissolved oxygen (DO) is the most important factor according to the expert ranking, as it is essential for respiration, aerobic metabolism, and normal growth. Seawater temperature strongly influences metabolic activity, feed utilization, growth rate, and physiological processes, while salinity affects osmoregulation and the maintenance of internal ionic balance. Therefore, substantial deviations in these three factors can create physiological stress and reduce the suitability of the culture environment.

pH is also an important indicator of the chemical stability of the aquatic environment. Appropriate pH conditions support normal physiological functions and help maintain suitable conditions for respiration and metabolism. Nutrient-related compounds, including ammonium (NH<sub>4</sub><sup>+</sup>-N) and phosphate (PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3-</sup>-P), are included because excessive concentrations may indicate nutrient enrichment and contribute to deterioration of water quality. High nutrient concentrations can promote excessive algal growth and subsequent oxygen depletion, thereby creating unfavorable conditions for cobia. TSS is another important water-quality factor because excessive suspended solids can increase turbidity and interfere with feeding and gill function.

Fluoride (F<sup>-</sup>) and cyanide (CN<sup>-</sup>) are considered potential toxic substances in the aquatic environment. Elevated concentrations may interfere with physiological processes and adversely affect fish health. Heavy metals, including As, Cd, Pb, Cr(VI), total chromium, Cu, Zn, Mn, and Fe, are also considered potential contaminants. Although some elements such as Cu, Zn, Mn, and Fe are essential micronutrients at appropriate concentrations, excessive levels may cause toxic effects, physiological stress, and disruption of metabolic processes. In contrast, elements such as As, Cd, and Pb are primarily considered contaminants because of their potential toxicity and accumulation in aquatic organisms.

Oil and grease are included as indicators of petroleum-related contamination. Elevated concentrations can adversely affect water quality and may interfere with respiration, feeding, and other physiological processes of fish. Overall, the expert-defined thresholds in the table above provide the basis for characterizing the environmental conditions of *R. canadum* into unsuitable, suitable, and highly suitable levels for the subsequent suitability scoring and classification process.
